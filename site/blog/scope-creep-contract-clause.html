Scope creep contract clause: example wording and how to use it

Published 2026-11-02 · SignedChange Guides

Scope creep is when a project slowly grows beyond what was agreed — one extra page, one more revision, one "quick" feature — without the price or deadline changing. It's one of the main reasons freelancers and agencies lose money on projects they thought were profitable.

A clear scope creep clause in your contract sets expectations from the start. Below are example clauses you can adapt, and a simple process to enforce them.

What a good scope clause does

A scope clause should do three things: describe exactly what's included, state that anything else is extra, and explain how extra work gets approved and priced. The third part is the one most people forget, and it's the one that actually prevents arguments.

Example clause 1: Simple version

Scope of work. The services are limited to those listed in Schedule A. Any work not listed in Schedule A ("Additional Work") will only be carried out after both parties sign a written change order describing the Additional Work, its price and any change to the timeline.

Example clause 2: With a rate for small changes

Changes. The Client may request changes to the scope at any time. Small changes estimated under 2 hours will be billed at $[rate] per hour. Larger changes will be quoted separately. No Additional Work will start until the Client approves the price and timeline in a signed change order. Any change may move the delivery date.

Example clause 3: Revisions limit

Revisions. The fee includes [two] rounds of revisions on each deliverable. A round of revisions means one consolidated list of changes sent at the same time. Further rounds are billed at $[amount] each.

These are examples, not legal advice. Adapt them to your work and check with a local professional for important contracts.

Write Schedule A carefully

The clause is only as strong as the scope description it points to. List deliverables, quantities, formats, number of pages or screens, revision rounds and what's explicitly excluded. "Website" invites scope creep; "5-page website: Home, About, Services, Blog index, Contact; content supplied by client" does not.

How to enforce the clause without losing the client

When a request falls outside scope, respond quickly and positively: acknowledge the idea, say it's outside the agreed scope, give a price and timeline, and send a change order to sign. For a step-by-step approach, read what to do when a client changes the project scope.

The key is consistency. If you do one small extra for free without recording it, the client learns that extras are free. If you want to be generous, still write it down and mark it "no charge (goodwill)". That keeps the scope clear.

Why clients cause scope creep (and why it's rarely malicious)

Most scope creep isn't a client trying to cheat you. People often don't know how much work a "small" request involves. A new button on a website can need design, development, testing and content. Moving an electrical socket can mean opening a wall. Clients also discover what they want as they see the work take shape, which is natural.

Understanding this changes how you respond. Instead of treating each request as a threat, treat it as a chance to explain the effort involved and offer a price. Clients who understand the cost usually accept it, or decide the change isn't worth it, which is also fine. The clause simply gives you a polite, agreed framework for that conversation.

Agencies with several team members benefit even more: a written process stops individual team members from quietly agreeing to extras that nobody bills for.

When scope creep has already happened

If the project has already grown, pause and reset. List what was originally agreed, what's been added, and propose a fair price for the additions. Get that list signed before continuing. If the client refuses to pay for work already done, see client won't pay for extra work.

Frequently asked questions

What is scope creep?

Scope creep is the gradual growth of a project's requirements beyond what was originally agreed, usually without a matching increase in budget or time.

Is a scope clause enough on its own?

No. It sets the rule, but you also need to follow it: record every change in a signed change order before doing the extra work.

Should I charge for every small change?

That's your choice. Many freelancers include a small buffer or a revision limit. What matters is that changes are recorded, even when free.

Can I add a scope clause to an existing project?

You can propose one as an amendment that both sides sign. Explain that it's to keep things clear for both of you.
