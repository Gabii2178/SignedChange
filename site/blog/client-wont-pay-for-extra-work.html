Client won't pay for extra work: what to do next

Published 2026-11-02 · SignedChange Guides

You agreed on a job. Halfway through, the client asked for "just one more thing". You said yes, did the work, and sent the invoice. Now they say they never agreed to pay for it, or that it was "included". This is one of the most common money problems freelancers, contractors and small agencies face, and it almost always starts the same way: a change was agreed by voice or chat, but never written down and signed.

This guide walks through what you can do right now to get paid, and how to make sure the next extra request never turns into an argument.

Step 1: Stay calm and collect what you already have

Before you send an angry message, gather every piece of evidence that the extra work was requested. Look for WhatsApp or SMS messages, emails, voice notes, call logs, meeting notes, photos of the site before and after, and any message where the client thanked you for the extra work. Even an informal "yes go ahead" is useful.

Write a short timeline: the original agreement and price, the date the client asked for the extra work, what they asked for, what you did, and when you finished. A clear timeline makes the conversation factual instead of emotional.

Step 2: Separate the extra work on your invoice

If your invoice shows one total, the client can argue about the whole thing. Split it: the original scope at the original price, then the additional work as separate lines with dates and a short description. This makes it obvious that you are not changing the original deal; you are charging for something new that they asked for.

Attach or reference the evidence for each extra line. A message like "Can you also paint the hallway?" next to the hallway line is hard to argue with.

Step 3: Have a direct, polite conversation

Call or message the client and describe the facts: "On 12 March you asked me to add the hallway. I did it on 14 March. That work is billed separately at the rate we use for extra work." Avoid accusations. Many clients genuinely forget, or assumed small extras were free.

If they agree to pay part of it, take the partial payment and put the agreed amount in writing straight away. A signed note of what was agreed today is worth more than a long chat history. You can create one in a minute with SignedChange and send them a link to sign on their phone.

Step 4: Send a formal written reminder

If the conversation doesn't work, send a short written reminder. State the amount, what it is for, the evidence, a due date (for example 7 or 14 days) and how to pay. Keep the tone professional. This message itself becomes evidence that you asked properly.

If you work through a platform such as Upwork or Fiverr, use their dispute process instead, and keep all communication inside the platform. For a deeper look at disputes, see our guide to handling a freelance payment dispute.

Step 5: Decide whether to escalate

If the amount is large, options in many countries include mediation, a small-claims court, or a lawyer's letter. Each costs time and sometimes money, so compare that cost with the amount owed. The rules differ by country, so check with a local professional before you start.

Sometimes the smartest business decision is to accept a smaller settlement, get it signed, and move on, while making sure it never happens again.

How to stop this happening again

The real fix is simple: never start extra work until the change is written down and signed by both sides. It doesn't need to be a long contract. A short record that says what is being added, the price, and the date, signed by you and the client, is enough to remove most arguments.

This is called a change order or a contract amendment. You can read how to write a change order, or use a ready-made template in the SignedChange app. It takes about 60 seconds and the client signs on their own phone.

It also helps to add a scope-change clause to your original agreement, so the client knows from day one that extras are billed. Our article on the scope creep contract clause includes example wording you can adapt.

A real-world example

A painter agreed to paint three rooms for $600. On day two, the owner asked him to "quickly do the hallway and the doors too". He agreed by voice, did the work over two extra days and invoiced $900. The owner refused to pay more than $600, saying the hallway was "obviously part of the job". With no written change, it became his word against hers, and he settled for $700.

Compare that with the same job where, before touching the hallway, he spends one minute sending a change note: "Add hallway and 4 doors, +$300, +2 days." The owner signs on her phone. At the end, the invoice shows $600 plus change order CO-1 for $300, and there's nothing to argue about. Same work, same client, very different outcome.

A simple script for the next "while you're here" request

When a client asks for more, try: "Sure, I can do that. It's outside what we agreed, so it will be an extra $X and add about two days. I'll send you a quick change note to sign, and I'll start as soon as it's signed." Most clients respect this. The ones who push back are exactly the clients who would have refused to pay later.

Saying this every time feels awkward at first, but it quickly becomes routine, and it protects the relationship as much as your income.

Frequently asked questions

Can I charge for extra work that wasn't in the contract?

Usually you can charge if the client clearly requested the work and you can show it. The challenge is proof. Messages, emails and especially a signed change record make it far easier to get paid.

What if the client says the extra work was included?

Compare the request with the original scope. If the original agreement lists what is included, show that the extra item is not on it. If the original scope was vague, that is a lesson for next time: describe scope precisely and record every change.

Should I stop work until I'm paid?

It depends on your agreement and local law. Pausing new or extra work until a change is signed is common and reasonable. Stopping agreed work may have consequences, so check your contract first.

Is a WhatsApp message enough proof?

It can help a lot, but it is often incomplete: it may not state the price or exact scope. A short signed document that states both is much stronger.
