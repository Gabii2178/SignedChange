What to do when a client changes the project scope

Published 2026-11-02 · SignedChange Guides

Scope changes are normal. Clients learn as a project develops, priorities shift, and new ideas appear. The problem isn't the change itself; it's handling it informally. Here's a simple 5-step process that keeps you paid and keeps the client happy.

Step 1: Acknowledge the request positively

Start with yes-energy: "Good idea, let me look at what that involves." You're not committing to anything yet, and you avoid sounding defensive. Clients who feel heard are more willing to accept the price that comes next.

Step 2: Compare it with the agreed scope

Pull up your original agreement or quote. Is the request already included, a small adjustment, or genuinely new work? If your original scope was vague, this step is harder, which is why a precise scope description matters. Our scope creep clause guide shows how to write one.

Step 3: Work out the impact

Estimate three things: extra (or reduced) cost, extra time, and any effect on other parts of the project. Changes often have knock-on effects: a new page needs design, copy and testing; moving a wall affects electrics and finishing. Be honest and specific.

Step 4: Put the change in writing and get it signed

Write a short change order: what changes, the price impact, the new total, the schedule impact, and a note that everything else stays the same. Send it for signature immediately. A change order template makes this quick.

With SignedChange, you can pick a template, enter the change and price, and send a signing link by WhatsApp or email. The client signs on their phone; you both get the PDF. Tips on getting a fast "yes" are in how to get a client to sign off on changes.

Step 5: Update your plan and invoice

Once signed, update your timeline, task list and invoice. Reference the change order number on the invoice line so it's obvious where every charge comes from.

What if the client removes work?

Treat a reduction the same way: record what's removed and the new price. If you've already done some of that work or bought materials, include that cost. Clear records protect you either way.

What if the change makes the project impossible?

Sometimes a change breaks the original plan entirely: a new platform, a totally different design, a bigger budget. In that case it may be cleaner to close out the original agreement (pay for work done) and agree a new one. Record that decision in writing too, possibly as a contract amendment.

Mistakes that turn scope changes into disputes

  • Saying "no problem" before checking the impact. Clients hear "free".
  • Giving a vague price. "A bit extra" will be remembered as "very little".
  • Starting before the change is signed. You lose leverage the moment the work is done.
  • Not updating the deadline. The client still expects the original date.
  • Bundling many small changes into one big surprise. Record each change as it happens, so the final invoice contains no shocks.

Avoiding these five mistakes covers most of the problems freelancers and contractors run into. The underlying habit is the same throughout: respond quickly, be specific about money and time, and get it signed before you act.

Clients who experience this process usually trust you more, not less, because they always know where the budget stands.

If you've already done unrecorded changes

It happens. Don't panic. List the changes made so far, propose a fair price, and ask the client to sign the list before continuing. If they refuse to pay, see client won't pay for extra work.

Frequently asked questions

Can a client change the scope without paying more?

Only if you agree. If the change adds work, it's reasonable to adjust the price and timeline, ideally as your contract already states.

How do I say no to a scope change?

Offer alternatives: do it in a later phase, swap it for something else in the current scope, or quote it separately.

Should small changes get a change order?

Yes, even if free. A quick record keeps the scope clear and prevents small changes piling up.

Who pays if the change delays the project?

That should be stated in the change order. Agree the new deadline when you agree the price.
