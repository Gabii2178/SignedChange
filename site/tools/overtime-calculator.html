Overtime Pay Calculator

Enter your hourly rate and the hours you worked this week. The calculator splits regular and overtime hours, applies time-and-a-half (or any multiplier you choose) and adds double time if you have any.

How overtime pay is calculated

Overtime is any time worked past the regular threshold in your pay period. In the United States, the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) sets that threshold at 40 hours in a workweek for non-exempt employees, and overtime must be paid at no less than one and a half times the regular rate. So if you earn $20 an hour and work 48 hours, you are paid 40 × $20 = $800 for regular time, plus 8 × $30 = $240 for overtime, for a gross of $1,040.

Some places use different rules. California, for example, also counts daily overtime after 8 hours in a day and requires double time after 12 hours in a day. Many other countries set their own weekly limits or premiums through law or collective agreements. That is why this calculator lets you change both the regular-hours threshold and the multiplier, and add a separate block of double-time hours.

Step-by-step example

  1. Find your regular hourly rate. If you also earn non-discretionary bonuses or shift differentials, your "regular rate" for overtime can be higher than your base rate.
  2. Count every hour you actually worked, including short tasks before and after your shift.
  3. Subtract the regular threshold (usually 40) to get your overtime hours.
  4. Multiply overtime hours by your rate and the multiplier.
  5. Add regular pay and overtime pay for your gross pay before tax.

Why overtime disputes happen

Most overtime arguments are not about the maths. They start when a shift is extended by a quick word in the hallway, a text at 9 pm, or a manager saying "can you just stay until close?" Weeks later nobody remembers who agreed to what, at what rate, and whether the extra hours were approved. Payroll goes by the schedule on file, and the worker goes by memory.

The fix is simple: write the change down at the moment it happens and get both people to confirm it. A dated record that says "Saturday shift added, 6 hours, paid at 1.5×, approved by the manager" is very hard to argue with later. Keep a copy of your original schedule, screenshot any messages that change it, and log your real start and end times every day.

Tips to make sure you are paid correctly

  • Check your pay stub every period against your own hours log.
  • Ask whether you are classified as exempt or non-exempt; job titles alone do not decide this.
  • Get any new schedule, extra shift or change in duties confirmed in writing before you work it.
  • If something is wrong, raise it quickly and in writing, attaching your records.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate time and a half?

Multiply your hourly rate by 1.5. Then multiply that overtime rate by the number of overtime hours. For a $20 rate, time and a half is $30 an hour.

When does overtime start?

Under the US FLSA it starts after 40 hours in a workweek for non-exempt employees. Some states and countries also have daily limits, so change the regular-hours field to match your rules.

Is overtime calculated before or after tax?

Overtime is part of your gross pay. This calculator shows gross pay before tax and deductions.

Can my employer change my hours without notice?

It depends on your contract and local law. Whatever the rules, ask for any change to your hours or pay to be confirmed in writing so there is a dated record.

Does the calculator store my data?

No. Everything runs in your browser and nothing you type is sent anywhere.
